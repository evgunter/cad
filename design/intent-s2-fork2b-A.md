# FORK-2b — what the product is, when no node replaces another

## For Ev

**Recommendation (likely): the product is the set of copies placed relative to the world. A placement is a record of its own, one row per copy: `(body, offset)`, the same shape an instance's gauge-and-offset already has. Inserting a body places nothing; reading a body places nothing; nothing derives a placement from what reads what. The explicit list goes, and so does every rule that moves it. One choice is yours: who writes the everyday placements (below).**

**Terms.** A *body* is a shape, a `Body` variable; it has no location. The *drawing space* of a document is where its datums and sketches are authored; a body built from them has coordinates there, and so does a boolean of two such bodies, which is why "which space is it in" cannot decide what appears. A *placement* is a row `Place { body, gauge, offset }`: a copy of that body sits on that gauge (the world when none) at that offset. A body is *placed* when a row names it, directly or through a mate chain to a placed copy (A11's group rule, unchanged). The *product* is the placed copies, each at its world pose, in row order.

**What this makes true.**
- "This is there" is said one way, as D10 wants: a placement. The product is read off the placement rows and nothing else. A body with no row is a value other operations read, and it appears nowhere: not in export, not in the gather, not in an instantiating document.
- A boolean is a third body. `C = A ∪ B` reads A and B, which must share a space (D10's rule, met by construction in a part document). C has coordinates in that space and no row; A and B keep whatever rows they had. The kernel replaces nothing.
- Two rows on one body are two copies (your 2026-10-03 words: "free to determine another placement in order to place two copies"). `PlacedUnderTwoRoots` retires; a mate on a twice-placed body names the copy, which A11's member identity already does for pattern copies. A `Transform` is a row with a non-identity offset, and a `Pattern` is a rule for rows; folding them in is stage 3's, not this fork's.
- Order is row order, a function of the edit sequence (D9). Nothing reads it but STEP's solid sequence and the gather's per-solid origin, so there is no reorder door: `SetRoots`/`SetProduct` goes, and `Place`/`Unplace` are the only doors (unsure: cheap to add a whole-table edit later).
- Delete follows D10's one reader rule: a row naming a deleted body is a stranded reader, kept, typed, and the world's gather refuses naming the row ("unplace it or restore the body"). FORK-2's "the entry leaves, reported" rested on the list not being a reader; a placement row is one.

**The document with no world mate: the choice.** Nothing implicit places a body. Any implicit rule is a sink rule in a new coat: "placed unless something reads it" is A10, and "placed when the document places nothing" vanishes everything the moment one row is written. So a one-body part has its row because someone wrote it, and the question is only who writes it by default. The stored document is identical under both; this is an authoring-surface default, like a `Formula` lowering (VR6), never kernel data.

- **P, place on make, re-place on combine (my lean, likely).** The façade's constructors and the viewer's tools write the rows: `extrude(...)` inserts and places at the identity on the world; `boolean(a, b)` inserts, places the result, and unplaces every row of `a` and `b`; same for fillet, shell, transform. Each is one history action lowering to several logged edits that say what happened: "insert; unplace a; unplace b; place c". Opt-outs are `place=False` and `keep=True`. Every script, corpus file and viewer flow reads as today. Against it: your "remain unplaced" is not literal here; A and B were placed and the gesture unplaced them.
- **N, nothing places until asked.** Constructors write no row; the viewer draws every unplaced body as a placement display in its drawing-space coordinates, coloured apart (your 2026-10-03 picture, exactly). Export, instantiation and the gate see nothing until the user places C; A and B stay unplaced and visible until hidden. Literal to your words, and the two questions ("what shape", "where") are two acts. Against it: every one-body part needs a place gesture before it is a product, every script gains a line per product body, and the viewer's default picture after a boolean is C drawn on top of A and B.

**Export and the gate.** Export writes the placed copies, as today. Under either default an unplaced body is a routine state (every boolean operand), so export cannot refuse over "unplaced" as A11 (2) says now; it refuses an empty world, naming the unplaced bodies and groups as the recourse, and otherwise writes the world with a notice of what it left out. That narrows your ruling of 2026-09-29 (#3441, "STEP can complain about unplaced parts") from refusal to notice; the refusal was ruled when only a deleted gauge could unplace anything. Say if you want the refusal kept for instance groups alone; I would not, since the two states are one state. The at-rest gate (A5) gathers the placed copies and, as today, each unplaced mated group by itself; an unplaced lone body has nothing to gate. A mate's face is minted on the placed copy of its body; a face whose body no row places refuses `RefusedRef::Unplaced` in place of the walk up the consumers.

**Split, inline, Promote (A4).** A row moves with its body: the cut's rows become the part's product at their offsets, and the remainder gets one instance on the anchor at the empty offset, so no world pose moves. "A cut of unplaced material alone refuses" already says what a cut with no row does. Inline composes the instance's placement onto each row it brings back. Promote and Fold touch gauges, not rows: unchanged. "The instance takes the first one's place" goes; the instance's row is where split writes it.

**Python and the viewer.** `Doc.roots` becomes `Doc.placed`: the rows, body and offset. `DocEdit.place(body, offset=identity, gauge=None)` and `DocEdit.unplace(row)`. The root badge becomes a placed badge on the body's row; unplaced bodies draw as placement displays, display state no logic reads (A11 (2)'s own-space display, widened from groups to bodies).

**How this sits with D10's spaces and A11.** Unchanged in substance. A space still gives coordinates and is computed in its earliest member's frame; the world is still the frame copies relate to and export reads. What this adds is that relating a copy to the world is a row a part's own body can hold, not only an instance, so A11 (2)'s gauge and offset on `InstantiatePart` is one case of the row. Stage 3's "placements as mates" grows the row's `offset` into mates; stage 2 needs only the identity-offset rows.

**The migration goal.** Under P, unit C still leaves membership unchanged on every corpus file: the migration writes one row per root body, and the constructors' defaults reproduce today's membership at every later edit. Under N the corpus builders each add a place. Mates never were rows, so the guide's "a mate is a product root" goes either way.

**Premise check.** The direction holds, with one sharpening it needs to be true: "placed relative to the world" must mean a per-copy record, not membership of a space that touches the world. A boolean's result has coordinates in its operands' space, so if being in a world-related space were what appeared, A and B would appear beside C. Your 2026-10-03 "the operands are in a different space than their output" was reasoned from `Transform`, and for a boolean it would be a fiction hiding an identity relation; the per-copy row is what makes "the first two not appearing" literally true without it.
