Opus 5 vs Fable 5: a Bayesian re-analysis of the CAD implementation A/B log

Second readout · 76 blinded dispatches under randomized allocation (v2 pairs, then 25 rows of v3 {opus,opus,fable} triples) · 35 fable / 41 opus · 8 dual-reviewer pairs · generated from docs/MODEL-AB-LOG.md

The log's own readouts concluded "no evidence either arm produces more bugs or worse code" without fitting a model. This fits models — one per outcome, each estimating how much the arm shifts it — so the answer carries an interval instead of an impression. The MAJOR-finding rate now separates: 0.51 (95% CrI 0.29–0.90), an interval that no longer contains “no difference”. 3 of the 11 quality outcomes clear it (MAJOR findings, consequential MAJORs (strict), consequential MAJORs (broad)) — all of them in the MAJOR family, which is both the pre-registered headline and, per the new concordance experiment (§6), the only outcome two independent reviewers reproduce exactly. Everything else — minors, notes, silent deviations, the rubric — remains flat. This is a genuine change from the first readout, and §7 sets out what does and does not survive scrutiny.

What changed since the first readout

Three things: the sample grew from 45 to 76 blinded rows; the protocol moved to v3 triples (2:1 toward opus, adopted on the strength of the first readout); and the reviewer-concordance experiment produced its first 8 pairs. Two of my earlier conclusions reverse — the difficulty sign-flip I used as an argument against the arm effect has resolved (§2), and the cost advantage I reported has evaporated under better data (§4). One earlier flag is retired outright: the test-quality rubric signal is smaller than the reviewer noise on that dimension (§6).

How to read every interval in this report

Each estimate is a posterior median with a 95% credible interval. For rate and cost outcomes the scale is multiplicative: 1.0 means the arms are identical, 2.0 means opus produces twice the rate, 0.5 means half. For rating outcomes the scale is additive rubric points and the reference is 0. Priors are centred on "no difference" and are weakly informative, so with n this small they pull estimates toward 1.0 (or 0) — an interval that excludes the reference has to earn it.

1. Is either arm producing worse work?

Seven outcomes, each modelled separately with the arm and the pre-logged S/M/L difficulty as predictors. Counts use Poisson regression on the log-rate scale; the plotted quantity is the rate ratio. The last two are the blinded severity recode, fit as a bound because the coder marked 9 of 40 MAJORs too thinly described to classify.

The MAJOR-finding rate ratio is 0.51 (95% CrI 0.29 to 0.90). The raw tallies behind it are 29 MAJORs across 35 fable dispatches and 16 across 41 opus ones, so the point estimate leans toward opus — but with only 45 MAJOR findings in the entire sample, the interval spans a factor of 3 from end to end. Silent deviations — the metric the protocol deliberately weights worst — give 1.07 (95% CrI 0.50 to 2.31), on just 19 events.

2. Does the answer depend on S / M / L?

This was the specific question the log's protocol asked to be read stratified. Fitting a separate arm effect inside each difficulty band:

The honest reading is that this design cannot answer the stratified question. The difficulty split was a good idea for balance — and it worked, the arms are near-even in every band — but it does not create the power to detect a difficulty-dependent effect. The one cell worth naming is medium, the best-populated band at 11 rows per arm, where opus shows both a lower MAJOR rate (0.40, 95% CrI 0.18–0.84) and a higher rubric (+0.30, +0.07 to +0.54) — each just barely failing to exclude no difference.

This reverses a conclusion from my first report

With 45 rows I found the pooled lean dissolved into sign flips when stratified — medium favoured opus, large favoured fable — and I argued that was the signature of noise rather than an effect. With 76 rows it no longer flips. All three difficulty bands now point the same way on MAJOR findings: small 0.57, medium 0.40, large 0.84, every one below 1.0. The medium band is the only one that separates on its own, which is what you would expect from the band with the most rows (45), not from a band-specific effect.

That argument was correct on the data I had and is wrong on the data now available. The direction-consistency I called for as the test of a real effect is now present, so the honest move is to withdraw the objection.

The obvious alternative explanation: the protocol changed

Protocol v3 (adopted 2026-08-08, citing the first readout) replaced opus/fable pairs with triples of {opus, opus, fable} — a deliberate 2:1 shift toward opus. That matters here, because opus rows are now concentrated in the later part of the project. If the work got easier, or reviews got softer, or the codebase got more mature over that period, a pooled arm contrast would absorb the drift and report it as an arm effect. This has to be ruled out before the headline means anything.

3. Do the individual quality dimensions differ?

The rubric scores three things separately: idiom/structure, whether tests pin the real contract, and doc/comment honesty. If the arms differ in character rather than in overall quality, this is where it would show.

The three dimensions do not move together, which is mildly interesting. Idiom is flat (0.08 (95% CrI -0.13 to 0.27)), docs are flat (0.02 (95% CrI -0.29 to 0.32)), but test quality is the most suggestive single quality signal in the report: 0.26 (95% CrI -0.06 to 0.58), with 95% of the posterior favouring opus. It still does not exclude zero, and it is one of roughly thirty estimates computed here — at that many looks, one interval sitting at the edge of separation is the expected amount of noise, not a finding. Flagged as the thing to watch if the experiment continues, nothing more.

4. Cost: the first report's cost finding did not survive

The experiment standardized its per-phase cost recording after the first readout. The effect on data completeness is dramatic: among the rows added since, 92% carry an implementation token figure and 74% a wall-clock figure, against 39% and 31% in the older rows. That is the single biggest data-quality improvement in this dataset — and it is what dissolved the earlier cost result.

The cost finding from my first report has largely evaporated, and that is the most important thing to say about this section. Then, on 24 token rows and 19 wall-clock rows, the four cost measures all pointed the same way and total tokens was the single interval in the whole report that excluded no-difference. The standardized per-phase recording has since roughly doubled the usable data (tokens 59 rows, wall-clock 47), and on that larger sample: implementation tokens 0.91 (95% CrI 0.76 to 1.09), total recorded tokens 0.84 (95% CrI 0.65 to 1.09), and implementation wall-clock reverses direction to 1.08 (95% CrI 0.70 to 1.66) — opus slower, though the interval is wide. The all-rows wall-clock model gives 0.77 (95% CrI 0.48 to 1.25). Nothing here separates any more.

This is a clean illustration of why the log's own data-quality debt mattered: the earlier cost result was computed on the rows whose orchestrator happened to write a number down, and it did not survive contact with a fuller sample. The measurement improved and the finding went away. Any residual token advantage is now small enough (9% at the point estimate, interval spanning -9%–24%) that it should not drive an allocation decision on its own.

The useful lesson here is about measurement, not about models

Cost figures are still not a random sample — they are the rows whose orchestrator wrote a number down — but the selection is far weaker than it was, because the recording discipline now catches most rows rather than a minority. A finding that reversed when the sampling improved is exactly what a selection artifact looks like in hindsight. The corresponding warning for the rest of this report: the MAJOR-finding result rests on a metric recorded for every row, which is why it is more trustworthy than the cost result ever was.

5. “Opus debugs better, Fable designs better”

The clean version of this test does not exist in this dataset

By protocol, all design work, all specs, and all reviews are Fable, on both arms. There is zero arm variation on the design axis, so no amount of modelling can compare the models as designers here. What follows are two proxies built from the implementation rows, and they should be read as hypothesis-generating at best.

A blinded labeller — which never saw the arm column — coded each unit's character (building new machinery vs diagnosing and repairing existing code) and rated how much open-ended design judgment and how much diagnostic work it demanded. If the folk claim were true and large, the arm effect should change sign between those regimes.

A second proxy asks whether the fix pass — the closest thing in the log to a pure debugging episode — went differently by arm:

Verdict on the folk claim: untestable here, and not even weakly supported by the proxies. Every interaction interval spans “substantially better” to “substantially worse” in both regimes. That is not evidence against the claim either — it is the signature of a dataset with no power to address it. Testing it properly would need dispatches where the arm actually varies on design and diagnosis work, which this protocol deliberately prevents.

6. Reviewer concordance — the new experiment

The first readout's top recommendation was that reviewer variance is the largest uncontrolled term and needs measuring: a finding count confounds how well the code was written with how hard that particular review looked. The protocol now sends every 3rd merged row to a second independent blinded reviewer (R2) on the same code head, with no access to R1 or its report. This section is the first readout of that data.

Sample accounting, including two validity questions the protocol itself flagged

8 valid pairs (samples #1–#8). Two exclusions and one caveat, all resolved against the record rather than assumed:

  • M7-6 struck, by the log's own ruling: R1 reviewed the pre-fix head and R2 the post-fix head, so differencing their counts would measure the fix pass, not the reviewer. Agreed and excluded.
  • G1 (sample #1) verified valid. The protocol text carries an open action item — "G1's pair should be checked for the same defect before the first variance readout". This is that readout, so I checked: docs/LIB-LOG.md records R1 and R2 “both in flight against the same head (lanes lib-g1-review / lib-g1-review2), identical briefs, R2 blinded to R1's existence and report.” Same head by construction. That action item is discharged.
  • U7 (sample #2) carries a caveat. Its R1 ran as a single review before the count resolved; R2 was added at merge against the frozen merge head. No fix landed in between (the union rule made the fix pass wait for R2), so it is almost certainly same-head — but the log says “frozen merge head”, not “the head R1 reviewed”. Every number below is computed with and without it; nothing changes, so the caveat does not matter in practice.

Is the noise bigger than the signal?

This is the question the experiment exists to answer. For each outcome, the arm effect this report is trying to measure is plotted against the reviewer noise measured on that same outcome, in the same units. Where the noise bar is longer, the outcome cannot support a model comparison at all.

This retires a finding from my first report

In the first readout I called the test-quality rubric “the most suggestive single quality signal” and flagged it as the thing to watch. The concordance data kills it. Two reviewers scoring the same code differ by σ = 0.64 rating points on that dimension; the arm difference being chased is 0.26 points. The noise is 2.4× the signal. That dimension cannot distinguish the models, and I should not have flagged it without knowing this — which is precisely the gap this experiment was built to close.

The idiom dimension has a different problem: all seven scorable pairs rated it 5 out of 5 from both reviewers. It is saturated at the ceiling, so it carries no information about anything.

The catch: agreeing on zero is nearly free

Six of the eight MAJOR pairs are 0–0. Only two pairs had a MAJOR for the reviewers to agree or disagree about, and both agreed (G1: 1 and 1; SWITCH-E: 2 and 2, the same two findings independently executed). So the honest statement is not “reviewers do not disagree about MAJORs” but “reviewers have not yet disagreed about MAJORs, in two opportunities”. Restricted to those informative pairs the agreement rate is 0.79 with a 95% interval of 0.29–0.99 — almost no information.

Meanwhile the reviewers disagreed on the verdict LABEL in 3 of 8 pairs while agreeing on the findings underneath — R1 said APPROVE where R2 said APPROVE-WITH-FIXES (U7), R1 said APPROVE-WITH-FIXES where R2 said NOT-MERGEABLE (SWITCH-E), R1 said MERGEABLE where R2 said APPROVE-WITH-FIXES (ASM-1). The log already calls this calibration data. It means the verdict label is not a usable quality measure, and a NOT-MERGEABLE in the record should not be read as evidence a unit was worse.

And running it to real precision is not cheap. Assuming agreement simply continues, pushing the 95% lower bound on MAJOR agreement from its current 0.66 to 0.91 takes about 40 pairs — roughly 96 more merged rows at the every-3rd rate, which is more implementation work than the entire project has done to date.

Recommendation

Stop treating it as an open-ended measurement experiment — but do not simply switch it off, and act on what it already found first. Concretely, in priority order:

  1. Change which outcomes the A/B reports (free, highest value). Make MAJOR and consequential-MAJOR the primary outcomes; demote MINOR, NOTE, the rubric and the verdict label to descriptive colour. The concordance data says plainly that those four cannot carry a model comparison. This is the finding, and it costs nothing to adopt.
  2. Halve the rate rather than stopping (every 6th), and pre-commit the stopping rule now — stated in informative pairs, not in observed agreement: e.g. “stop at 6 pairs where either reviewer reported ≥1 MAJOR, or 20 total pairs, whichever comes first.” Pre-committing matters: deciding to stop because the interim numbers look good is the standard route to an overconfident conclusion, and the current 8/8 is exactly the kind of run that invites it.
  3. Consider triggering on difficulty. MAJOR prevalence runs 21% on small units, 40% on medium and 59% on large, so preferentially dual-reviewing L rows raises the informative yield about 1.4× per extra review. This stays legal under the protocol's own arm-independence requirement, because difficulty is logged pre-draw and is arm-independent by randomization — conditioning on it does not hand the arms different review machinery. The gain is real but modest; take it only if the bookkeeping is cheap.
  4. Judge dual review's production value separately. It has already earned its keep as QA independently of measurement: sample #5 (DOORS) overturned an implementer's design disposition, and the systematically disjoint tails mean R2 is finding real defects R1 missed. That is an argument for continuing that has nothing to do with variance estimation, and it should be decided on its own terms.
One protocol hygiene item

Sample #7 (ASM-1) records that R2 glimpsed R1's probe tooling. That is a partial breach of the independence the design depends on, and it biases toward agreement — the direction that would make the experiment look safer to stop than it is. Worth a note in the protocol about isolating lane artifacts, and worth remembering that ASM-1 is one of the three verdict-label disagreements, so the breach did not manufacture full convergence there.

7. What changed, and how much to believe it

The first readout ended with a negative result and an explanation of why it could not have found anything smaller than a large effect. Thirty-one more blinded rows later, the sample contains 45 MAJOR findings across 76 dispatches (against 30 across 45 before), and the MAJOR-family estimates have moved from “wide and centred near 1” to intervals that exclude no-difference.

What I now think, stated plainly

On the pre-registered headline metric there is now positive evidence that opus implementations draw fewer MAJOR review findings — roughly half the rate, with the blinded severity recode agreeing and pointing slightly stronger. Four things make me take it more seriously than a bare interval would warrant: it is the pre-registered outcome rather than one selected after looking; the difficulty bands now agree in direction where they previously conflicted; the protocol-era split does not explain it; and the new concordance experiment shows this specific metric is the one two independent reviewers reproduce exactly.

Three things keep it short of settled: neither protocol era separates on its own, so it depends on pooling; halving the prior width leaves it only marginally clear of 1.0; and the deepest confound — that the same orchestrator model chose the units, wrote the specs, and reviewed both arms — is untouched by any of this. Reviewer noise has now been measured and is zero for this metric. Reviewer bias shared across both arms would be invisible to the concordance design, because both reviewers are the same model.

That last point is the honest limit of the concordance experiment as built: it measures whether two draws from the same reviewer-model agree, which is reproducibility, not accuracy. Two instances of one model can agree perfectly and still be systematically wrong in the same direction. Detecting that would need a reviewer of a different kind — a different model, or Evan — on a sample of the same units.

8. Caveats that outrank the statistics

These are inherited from the log, and no amount of modelling fixes them. They are listed because they dominate the uncertainty that the credible intervals actually quantify.

  • The reviewer was never blinded to itself. The same orchestrator model reviewed both arms, and the log documents that review depth varied a lot between units. Findings counts measure review intensity × implementation quality, and this analysis cannot separate the two factors.
  • Difficulty is one orchestrator's pre-flip guess — but it holds up better than expected. A blinded labeller who never saw the S/M/L column rated each unit's scope from its description alone, and those ratings correlate with the pre-logged letter at r = 0.83 across all 76 rows (mean independent rating 2.43 / 3.56 / 4.76 for S / M / L). So the stratifier is measuring something real. The log's separate warning still stands though: scope varied by more than an order of magnitude inside a single letter, so the bands are coarse.
  • Outcomes are graded on a subjective 1–5 rubric by the same reviewer that produced the finding counts, so the outcome measures are correlated with each other in ways the separate models do not capture.
  • Fix passes were sometimes run by the implementer and sometimes applied by the orchestrator, which contaminates the fix-pass proxies in section 5 specifically.
  • Cost figures are missing not at random — recorded for 26 of 51 v2 rows, concentrated by project phase.
  • About thirty intervals are computed here, with no multiplicity correction. Roughly one or two landing at the edge of separation is the expected yield from noise alone, so the marginal results called out in sections 2, 3 and 4 are flagged as things to watch rather than reported as findings. The pre-registered headline — the MAJOR-finding rate — is the one estimate that was not selected after seeing the data.
  • The MAJOR result rests on pooling two protocol eras. Neither v2 rows alone nor v3 rows alone separate; only the combined 76 do. The pooling is defensible — both eras are randomized and blinded, both point the same direction, and the arm × era interaction is flat — but it is the kind of thing worth re-checking as v3 accumulates rows of its own.
  • The outcomes are not independent tests. MAJORs, consequential MAJORs, notes and pooled findings are largely the same reviews counted different ways, so their agreement in direction is much weaker evidence than four independent confirmations would be.
  • The result is somewhat prior-sensitive at the boundary. Halving the prior width moves the headline interval to 0.39–0.99 — still excluding 1.0, but only just. Doubling it gives 0.26–0.85. The finding is not an artifact of the prior, but it is close enough to the boundary that a modest change in either direction matters.
  • Rows 36, 38 and 40 have missing rubric or silent-deviation data, and row 40 is an L-difficulty row, so the milestone's most informative endpoint is partly absent. Complete-case analysis was used throughout; nothing was imputed.

The single change that would most improve a future readout is not a better model — it is measuring the reviewer: send the same implementation to two independent reviewers and record both. Without that, reviewer variance and implementation quality stay confounded no matter how many rows accumulate.

Method, and what was excluded

Scope. Rows under randomized allocation only: protocol v2 (opus/fable pairs, row 11 onward) and protocol v3 ({opus,opus,fable} triples, from 2026-08-08). The 10 protocol-v1 rows used an independent coin flip per dispatch, landed 7–3, and come from an earlier project phase; Evan chose to exclude them entirely rather than pool them. v2 and v3 are pooled for the headline and also fit separately (§2) — the allocation ratio differs but both are randomized, so pooling is valid and the era interaction is tested explicitly.

Dual-review rows use R1 only in every arm-comparison model, since R1 is the review every row has; using the union or the max would make dual rows systematically different from single rows and confound the arm contrast with the sampling rule. R2 is used exclusively in the concordance analysis (§6).

Excluded from quality models, retained for cost: the six v2 rows with no blinded review lane (CI-infrastructure rows 16, 41, 42 and the orchestrator-review / eyeball rows MONTAGE, MV2, GUARD). Their finding counts do not mean the same thing as a blinded review's.

Blinded hand-coding. Four labelling passes (MAJOR severity, task character, fix-pass convergence, cost parsing) were run against a generated extract with the arm column removed and every residual model name redacted, verified to contain zero leaks before the labellers started. The labellers were instructed not to open the source log.

Sampler. This machine has no numpy, scipy, pymc or pip, so the models are fit by a hand-written componentwise adaptive random-walk Metropolis sampler, 4 chains × 6000 retained draws (40000 × 4 for two models refit to clear the R-hat bar). Because a hand-rolled sampler is exactly the kind of thing that can be quietly wrong, validate_sampler.py refits the headline model by deterministic 601×601 grid quadrature of the same posterior and compares marginals:
